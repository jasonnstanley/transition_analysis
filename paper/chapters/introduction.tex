\section{Introduction}
The transition from secondary school to first-year university mathematics remains a significant challenge for many students entering science, engineering, and related disciplines. Differences in prior mathematical preparation, curriculum pathways, and academic readiness contribute to wide variation in student success during the first year of university study. For institutions, understanding these transition pathways is important not only for improving student outcomes, but also for informing curriculum design, targeted support programs, and evidence-based educational practice. This study is motivated by the need to better understand the factors associated with successful transition into first-year university mathematics and to provide insights that can support both students and educators.

\paragraph Over the past two decades, considerable research has examined factors associated with success in first-year university mathematics, including prior academic achievement, secondary school mathematics background, demographic characteristics, and institutional measures of academic preparedness. More recently, advances in educational data analytics and machine learning have enabled increasingly sophisticated predictive models of student performance. While these approaches have improved predictive capability, there remains a need for analyses that not only identify influential predictors but also interpret their relative importance in ways that are meaningful for educational decision-making. This study addresses that need through a transparent and reproducible analytical framework that combines predictive modelling with educational interpretation to better understand transition pathways into first-year university mathematics.
\paragraph This paper investigates factors associated with successful transition into a first-stage undergraduate mathematics subject using a reproducible analytical workflow that integrates statistical analysis, machine learning, independent verification, and automated reporting. Rather than relying on a single predictive model, multiple tuned decision tree and random forest models are compared to evaluate both predictive performance and the consistency of feature importance across modelling approaches. By combining robust analytical methods with educational interpretation, the study aims not only to identify predictors of student success but also to provide practical insights that may inform curriculum design, transition support, and future research in first-year mathematics education.
