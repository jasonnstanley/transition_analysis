\section{Introduction}

The transition from secondary school to first-year university mathematics remains a significant challenge for many students entering science, engineering, and related 
disciplines. Differences in prior mathematical preparation, curriculum pathways, and academic readiness contribute to wide variation in student success during the first 
year of university study. For institutions, understanding these transition pathways is important not only for improving student outcomes, but also for informing curriculum 
design, targeted support programs, and evidence-based educational practice. This study is motivated by the need to better understand the factors associated with successful 
transition into first-year university mathematics and to provide insights that can support both students and educators.

Over the past two decades, considerable research has examined factors associated with success in first-year university mathematics, including prior academic achievement, 
secondary school mathematics background, demographic characteristics, and institutional measures of academic preparedness. More recently, advances in educational data 
analytics and machine learning have enabled increasingly sophisticated predictive models of student performance. While these approaches have improved predictive capability, 
there remains a need for analyses that not only identify influential predictors but also interpret their relative importance in ways that are meaningful for educational 
decision-making. This study addresses that need through a transparent and reproducible analytical framework that combines predictive modelling with educational interpretation 
to better understand transition pathways into first-year university mathematics.

This paper presents a reproducible framework for analysing student transition pathways into first-stage undergraduate mathematics through an integrated workflow combining 
statistical analysis, interpretable machine learning, independent verification, and automated reporting. As a case study, the framework is applied to investigate factors 
associated with successful transition into a first-stage undergraduate mathematics subject using multiple tuned decision tree and random forest models. Rather than advocating 
a single predictive model, the study emphasises consistency of interpretation across modelling approaches and demonstrates how transparent, reproducible analytical workflows 
can support educational research. The principal contribution is therefore not a new predictive algorithm, but a reusable research framework that integrates data preparation, 
model development, independent verification, interpretation, and publication-ready reporting within a single reproducible pipeline.
